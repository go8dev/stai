## Abstract

We investigate the predictive power of candlestick patterns for short-term cryptocurrency price movements. Using hourly OHLC data from the period 07/2018 to 01/2022 for nearly 400 cryptocurrencies, 2000 trading pairs, and 36 exchanges (200 million observations), we examine 55 reversal patterns identified via python’s TA-Lib package. Certain bullish patterns, such as the Bullish Harami and Hikkake, precede statistically significant positive returns, while bearish patterns, including the Bearish Harami and Hanging Man, anticipate significant declines. Profitability is assessed using the stepwise superior predictive ability test to mitigate data-snooping, supported by several robustness checks. Results remain consistent across time, market conditions, cryptocurrencies, and exchanges, and are unaffected by volume or volatility. Our findings offer robust evidence that some candlestick patterns can serve as reliable tools for cryptocurrency market forecasting.

## 1\. Introduction

In recent years, numerous YouTube channels dedicated to cryptocurrency have amassed large followings, boasting hundreds of thousands of subscribers and equally impressive view counts per video. A significant portion of these channels specializes in providing content focused on price forecasts for specific coins and tokens. Many rely on the analysis of charts, which they discuss in detail during their broadcasts. Some channels, such as [Benjamin Cowen on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/@intothecryptoverse), with an audience of almost 800k subscribers as of January 2024, exclusively focus on content derived from chart analysis, particularly technical analysis. Moreover, charts and associated tools are readily accessible to investors on various websites, including coingecko.com, coinmarketcap.com, and tradingview.com, among others. The cryptocurrency market, over the period of 2018 to early 2022, exhibited a market capitalization ranging from approximately USD 100 billion to three trillion. The daily trading volume reached up to USD 534 billion, accounting for 30% of the total market capitalization (as of May 19th, 2021, according to coingecko.com). Given these substantial figures, it is standing to reason that technical analysis plays a pivotal role in cryptocurrency markets, informing transactions potentially worth billions of dollars on a daily basis. This raises the fundamental question of the efficacy of investigating the historical performance of cryptocurrencies. In other words, does technical analysis yield profitable outcomes in cryptocurrency markets? If so, it would challenge Fama’s Efficient Market Hypothesis. Conversely, if not, the motivation behind the significant efforts invested by the cryptocurrency community and corporate platforms in examining price charts comes into question. This paper addresses precisely the issue of the profitability of trading strategies based on candlestick charts. In contrast to existing literature, our study focuses on an extensive dataset comprising almost 2000 different trading pairs from numerous cryptocurrency exchanges worldwide. Furthermore, the data is collected at an hourly frequency, enabling a detailed examination of short-term price movements following candlestick patterns.

Researchers, particularly practitioners, have recognized for decades that Japanese candlestick charts serve as a valuable graphical tool, offering comprehensive information in a single view. These charts provide data on open, high, low, and close (OHLC) prices for an asset within a specified trading interval. However, proponents such as ( [Murphy, 1999](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#b39)) argue that candlesticks go beyond merely presenting prices. They assert that when candles manifest in specific shapes, formations, or sequences, they convey additional insights. In the literature, these configurations are termed _candlestick patterns_. Advocates of candlestick analysis contend that the appearance of certain patterns in charts can predict subsequent trends and price movements. Despite the plethora of known candlestick patterns, it is essential to recognize that there are essentially two categories: _continuation patterns_ and _reversal patterns_. Continuation patterns indicate that a prevailing trend, whether upward or downward, is poised to persist. Conversely, reversal patterns suggest that the ongoing trend is on the verge of a reversal. The latter signifies the conclusion of an existing trend and identifies optimal entry points for exposure to the respective asset. Should the anticipated trend reversal materialize, trading the pattern by assuming a long or short position in the asset can lead to a profitable outcome. This study aims to comprehensively analyze the predictive power of candlestick reversal patterns in the global cryptocurrency market, leveraging a substantial set of intraday data.

Candlestick patterns fall within the realm of _technical analysis (TA)_, a discipline that relies _exclusively on market data_, such as prices or trading volume. While TA incorporates a diverse range of indicators, [1](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#fn1) it is essential to view TA as a supplementary approach to fundamental analysis. Fundamental analysis, which bases investment decisions on financial reports such as a company’s balance sheet or income statement, remains a cornerstone in decision-making. However, certain assets, particularly cryptocurrencies like bitcoin, lack traditional fundamentals. Bitcoin, for instance, does not have a balance sheet or an income statement. Consequently, investors in cryptocurrencies primarily rely on technical analysis before making investment decisions, leading to the widespread popularity of TA in cryptocurrency markets. In this context, where fundamental data may be scarce or nonexistent, technical analysis serves as a critical tool for assessing and making informed decisions within the dynamic landscape of cryptocurrency investments.

Another distinctive aspect of cryptocurrencies is their strong reliance on community-driven dynamics (for instance, [Meyer et al. (2023)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#b37) and [Moser and Brauneis (2023)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#b38) explore the role of YouTube in cryptocurrency price dynamics, [Choi (2021)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#b11) and [Kraaijeveld and De Smedt (2020)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#b30) reveal the predictive power of twitter content for cryptocurrency returns, and [Aslanidis et al. (2022)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#b4) and [Bleher and Dimpfl (2019)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#b7) disclose informational content of google trends volume on subsequent crypto market data). Consequently, it is not uncommon to observe discussions about cryptocurrency price trends across various social media platforms. On platforms like YouTube, analysts often conduct live-streams where they draw trend lines and identify candlestick patterns in real-time, supporting their speculative insights into future price developments. Notably, prominent crypto analysts boast millions of followers or subscribers on their social media channels, suggesting that their audience – potential crypto investors or traders – is likely familiar with these patterns, or at least the most commonly recognized ones. Given the substantial following of these analysts and the emphasis on ’doing your own research’ (DYOR) before making investment decisions, we assume that followers may utilize these patterns as trading signals. Consequently, the appearance of these patterns on a chart could potentially lead to price movements. In support of this notion, [Moser and Brauneis (2023)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#b38) demonstrate that cryptocurrency prices do respond when highly influential figures in the crypto space cover specific coins, especially those with a low market capitalization.

Extensive research has been conducted on candlestick patterns and their informational content in traditional financial markets, particularly in equity markets. In Section [2](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#sec2), we will discuss existing works and review relevant literature in this domain. However, the exploration of candlestick patterns in cryptocurrency markets remains relatively limited. This study aims to address this research gap by providing a comprehensive analysis. While we identified two (partially) comparable studies, the literature on cryptocurrency markets is still sparse. One notable study by [Cohen (2021)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#b12) focused on bitcoin data and examined the occurrence of three candlestick reversal patterns. Another comprehensive investigation was carried out by [Ho et al. (2021)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#b25), covering 23 cryptocurrencies and analyzing 68 candlestick patterns. Detailed insights into these studies are presented in Section [2](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#sec2). It is noteworthy that both studies utilized _daily data_ for their analyses. Given the rapid pace and high volatility characteristic of the cryptocurrency market, a higher data frequency is crucial for capturing its dynamics effectively. Consequently, our study uniquely contributes by examining the market at the _intraday level (i.e., 1-h)_, a perspective not explored in prior research. Additionally, our focus extends to the _broad_ cryptocurrency market, which is particularly relevant considering the vast landscape of digital assets. As of January 2024, more than 12,000 distinct cryptocurrencies are listed on coingecko.com (refer to [https://www.coingecko.com/](https://www.coingecko.com/) for further details).

While bitcoin continues to maintain its position as the leading cryptocurrency, the recent upsurge in the popularity of alternative cryptocurrencies, commonly known as altcoins, has significantly diversified the evolving market. This diversification, particularly notable during mid-2020, also referred to as the ’DeFi-Summer,’ underscores the need for research that extends beyond bitcoin. This study endeavors to fill this gap by offering a comprehensive exploration of alternative cryptocurrencies. To ensure a broad and inclusive analysis, we leverage data from 1935 cryptocurrency trading pairs sourced from 36 exchanges. This extensive dataset allows for a more nuanced understanding of the evolving landscape.

Motivated by these considerations, our study aims to address the following research questions:

- a.
Can the identification of reversal candlestick patterns serve as a reliable predictor of trend reversals in the expansive intraday cryptocurrency market?

- b.
Does the implementation of a corresponding trading strategy based on these patterns yield profitability?


The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. In Section [2](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#sec2) we give an overview of the existing research on candlestick patterns, in Section [3](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#sec3) we describe the data used in the empirical analyses, Section [4](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#sec4) outlines the methodology. The results are presented in Section [5](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#sec5), and Section [6](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#sec6) concludes.

## 2\. Literature review

Research in the domain of technical analysis (TA) spans across diverse fields, with a prevailing emphasis on _Technical Indicators_. Several comprehensive studies, such as those conducted by [Vo and Yost-Bremm (2020)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#b47), [Huang et al. (2019)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#b28), and [Anghel (2021)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#b3), explore this area, employing a plethora of up to 124 indicators and analyzing a substantial dataset comprising 816 cryptocurrencies. These analyses are often conducted at a granular level, such as the 1-minute interval. Given the exhaustive exploration of technical indicators in existing literature, our study distinguishes itself by focusing on a less-explored facet of TA, specifically, the intricate realm of candlestick patterns.

The profitability of candlestick patterns in traditional financial markets remains a subject of ongoing debate. While [Caginalp and Laurent (1998)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#b10) and [Lu et al. (2012)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#b32) present empirical evidence supporting the predictive power of these patterns, [Marshall et al. (2006)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#b36) and [Lucke (2003)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#b33) provide contrasting viewpoints, challenging the efficacy of such patterns. Despite a wealth of research on candlestick patterns in stocks, futures, or FX markets (e.g., [Rink, 2023](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#b44), [Park & Irwin, 2007](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#b42), [Marshall et al., 2008](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#b35), [Horton, 2009](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#b26), [Duvinage et al., 2013](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#b15)), there is a noticeable scarcity of literature specifically examining their effectiveness in the context of the cryptocurrency market. Notably, [Cohen (2021)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#b12) and [Ho et al. (2021)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#b25) contribute to this niche, providing valuable insights into the predictive power of classical candlestick patterns in the realm of cryptocurrencies. Our study extends this line of research by seeking to evaluate the profitability of candlestick patterns at a higher frequency, specifically, on a 1-h interval. Building upon the observations made by [Ho et al. (2021)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#b25), who attribute the ineffectiveness of candlestick patterns in cryptocurrency trading to the high volatility inherent in these markets, we aim to investigate whether a higher frequency level can capture patterns that may lead to profitable trading strategies.

A few studies have explored the incorporation of candlestick data in cryptocurrency markets, with notable examples being [Day et al. (2021)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#b14) and [El Youssefi et al. (2023)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#b17). However, it is noteworthy that these studies often diverge from the traditional Japanese candlestick patterns described extensively by [Murphy (1999)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#b39). These studies rather focus on exit strategies and exit timing as well as on clustering techniques - e.g., by leveraging the elbow method - using candlestick charts. Our focus on classical patterns - such as Harami, Doji or Abandoned Baby - aims to contribute a nuanced perspective to the existing body of literature.

Furthermore, recent studies in the cryptocurrency domain showcase an increasing trend toward utilizing big data. This rise in data volume has motivated researchers to leverage sophisticated tools, including artificial intelligence and machine learning, for precise price prediction. Notable works in this direction include studies by [Nakano et al. (2018)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#b40), [Alonso-Monsalve et al. (2020)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#b2), [Alkhodhairi et al. (2021)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#b1), [Farias (2022)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#b19), [Gudavalli and Kancherla (2023)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#b21), and [Goutte et al. (2023)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#b20). While these studies are pivotal in advancing our understanding of predictive modeling in cryptocurrency markets, the main objective of our paper is distinct. We aim to contribute to the finance literature by specifically exploring the viability of intraday candlestick patterns in cryptocurrency markets. In doing so, we intend to provide practical and actionable candlestick-based trading rules that can be readily implemented by market participants, especially those who may not possess the technical skills required for employing advanced models from the artificial intelligence toolbox.

## 3\. Data

For the empirical study, we leverage data sourced from [www.cryptotick.com](http://www.cryptotick.com/), a reputable commercial provider of high-quality cryptocurrency data. The investigation spans a period of 1281 trading days, equivalent to 30,744 h, commencing from July 1, 2018, 00:00 UTC, to January 1, 2022, 24:00 UTC. The dataset encompasses hourly observations for open (O), high (H), low (L), and close (C) prices, along with trading volume (V) and the total number of transactions (Tx).

At the onset of the dataset, we identify 8598 trading pairs listed on 74 exchanges. By July 1, 2021, coinciding with the period preceding the all-time-high of bitcoin and several other cryptocurrencies, these numbers surged to 24,920 trading pairs on 135 exchanges. This growth is attributed to the introduction of new currencies and the establishment of new trading venues during the sample period. Notably, some trading pairs and venues were discontinued from coverage by [www.cryptotick.com](http://www.cryptotick.com/). Over the entire sample period, a total of 250 exchanges were available, with 45 enduring throughout the mid-2018 to end-of-2021 timeframe.

To be included in the final dataset, a trading pair must exhibit 75% or 23,058 out of 30,744 hourly observations for OHLC data. This requirement ensures an extended time period of simultaneously available data for any two time series, preventing analyses on trading pairs with mutually exclusive time periods and potentially different characteristics. Adhering to this rule reduces the sample to 2356 trading pairs and 47 exchanges. Additionally, we apply a filter based on coinmarketcap.com’s and coingecko.com’s ratings of cryptocurrency spot exchanges as of April 2022, excluding unrated exchanges. This results in a dataset comprising 36 exchanges and 1935 trading pairs, as detailed in [Table 1](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#tbl1).

Regarding the geographic distribution of the trading venues, 10 exchanges are located in North and South America, 18 in Asia and Oceania, and 8 in Europe. Out of the 1935 trading pairs, the majority (1511) are traded in Asian venues, with over 1000 pairs listed on four Asia-based exchanges: Huobi Pro, Binance, Okex, and Kucoin. The dataset also includes 255 American and 169 European trading pairs. Furthermore, 794 out of 1935 trading pairs involve bitcoin, while 813 include fiat currencies or stable coins such as the US dollar, euro, USD coin, or tether. In summary, the study covers a diverse range of trading pairs listed on centralized cryptocurrency exchanges globally.

## 4\. Methodology

Our methodology entails a systematic four-step assessment of OHLC (Open, High, Low, Close) price data. Firstly, we identify candlestick patterns across the 1935 time series of trading pair data under consideration. To standardize our analysis, we rely on the python TA-Lib library, [2](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#fn2) an open-source technical analysis toolkit, available since 2001, and widely used in both academic research and industry. TA-Lib implements widely accepted fixed numerical thresholds for pattern components, thereby removing interpretive discretion from the researcher. Readers interested in further details may consult the GitHub repository at [https://github.com/TA-Lib/ta-lib/tree/main/src/ta\_func](https://github.com/TA-Lib/ta-lib/tree/main/src/ta_func), which contains the source code and pattern definitions. Secondly, we evaluate the prevailing trend (uptrend, downtrend, or no trend) preceding the occurrence of each identified pattern. Thirdly, upon the manifestation of a pattern, we scrutinize the subsequent price development and compare it to a benchmark. Finally, the statistical properties of returns following a specific pattern are examined through the application of the skewness adjusted t-test as well as the superior predictive ability test. Subsequent subsections provide detailed insights into each of these steps.

### 4.1. Patterns

In the initial phase of our analysis, it is imperative to identify and timestamp the specific patterns warranting investigation. To achieve this, we leverage the TA-Lib Python library, focusing on the detection of both bullish and bearish _reversal_ patterns within our extensive dataset. In accordance with the approach advocated by [Nison (1994)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#b41), we concentrate solely on reversal patterns, excluding continuation patterns.

Our analysis involves the identification and examination of a total of 30 bullish and 25 bearish patterns. Notably, the patterns under scrutiny consist of up to three distinct candles. A pattern is considered completed with the final candle, and consequently, we timestamp the trading signal with the end of the hour featuring this last candle. For instance, if a three-candle ’Morning Star’ pattern materializes in the time periods {𝑡−2,𝑡−1,𝑡}, the signal is deemed completed at time 𝑡. Subsequently, returns from trading this signal are derived from close prices spanning from 𝑡 to 𝑡+1, 𝑡+1 to 𝑡+2 and so forth.

Similarly, if the OHLC price data indicates the occurrence of the one-candle ’Hammer’ pattern from 𝑡−1 to 𝑡, we consider the pattern complete at time 𝑡. The details of the identified patterns, along with the corresponding occurrences, are presented in [Table 2](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#tbl2), which will we discussed in greater detail in Section [5](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#sec5).

In totality, our dataset comprises approximately 200 million data points, encompassing open, high, low, and close prices from the 1935 trading pairs, each with an average of around 26,000 observations. This corresponds to 50 million individual candles shaped by hourly OHLC data. While certain patterns, such as the bullish and bearish Hikkake patterns, manifest frequently with over 1.5 million instances each, others are comparatively rare, with fewer than 1000 instances. For example, the bullish reversal pattern ’Concealing Baby Swallow’ is observed in only 392 instances overall. Despite the disparate frequencies, we consider these numbers sufficiently large for robust statistical analysis, and therefore, we retain all patterns in our sample. This inclusive approach ensures a comprehensive examination of the varied patterns, irrespective of their occurrence frequencies.

### 4.2. Trends

Among chart analysts, identifying distinctive and predictive trading signals necessitates considering both the reversal candlestick pattern and the prevailing trend that precedes the pattern simultaneously. A meaningful bullish reversal pattern aligns with a preceding downtrend, indicating the conclusion of the prevailing downtrend and a shift toward an uptrend. Conversely, the opposite holds true for bearish reversal patterns and uptrends. Therefore, diligent observation of the trend is essential to avoid misinterpretations of a pattern.

While the TA-Lib library provides pattern recognition functions, it lacks trend recognition capabilities. Consequently, it becomes necessary for us to define uptrend and downtrend independently. Following the methodology proposed by [Ho et al. (2021)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#b25), we define the prevailing 𝑛-period Moving Average at time 𝑡 as: (1)𝑀⁢𝐴𝑛⁡\[𝑡\]=1𝑛⁢𝑛−1∑𝑖=0𝑝𝑐𝑡−𝑖

Here, 𝑝𝑐𝑡 denotes the closing price at hour 𝑡. In our empirical analysis, we adopt a Moving Average over the past six days to discern the trend. In accordance with [Ho et al. (2021)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#b25), the prevailing trend at hour 𝑡 is identified as an uptrend if: (2)𝑀⁢𝐴𝑛⁡\[𝑡−6\]<𝑀⁢𝐴𝑛⁡\[𝑡−5\]<⋯<𝑀⁢𝐴𝑛⁡\[𝑡−1\]<𝑀⁢𝐴𝑛⁡\[𝑡\].

Conversely, a prevailing downtrend is defined as: (3)𝑀⁢𝐴𝑛⁡\[𝑡−6\]>𝑀⁢𝐴𝑛⁡\[𝑡−5\]>⋯>𝑀⁢𝐴𝑛⁡\[𝑡−1\]>𝑀⁢𝐴𝑛⁡\[𝑡\].

### 4.3. Trading strategy and benchmarks

In the subsequent phase, we explore the feasibility of converting trading signals based on candlestick patterns into actual profits. Operating at an hourly data resolution, our empirical analysis focuses on evaluating short-term price reactions following the emergence of these patterns. To gauge the _predictive power_ of the patterns, we examine returns for the 24 h immediately following the recognition of a bullish or bearish reversal pattern in the data, while considering the prevailing trend. We choose a 24-h window for our analysis since candlestick signals are generally considered valid for a maximum of 10 periods ( [Duvinage et al., 2013](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#b15)). Therefore, a 24-h window is deemed appropriate to capture all price effects induced by the signals.

The calculation of hourly returns for trading pair 𝑖 is given by: (4)𝑟𝑖,𝑡=𝑝𝑐𝑖,𝑡−𝑝𝑐𝑖,𝑡−1𝑝𝑐𝑖,𝑡

Here, 𝑝𝑐𝑖,𝑡 represents the closing price of trading pair 𝑖 at hour 𝑡, and 𝑝𝑐𝑖,𝑡−1 is the closing price from the preceding hour. Additionally, we evaluate cumulative returns in the 24 h after a pattern occurs, defined as: (5)𝑟𝑖,𝑡,ℎ=𝑝𝑐𝑡+ℎ−𝑝𝑐𝑡𝑝𝑐𝑡

This equation denotes the cumulative return 𝑟𝑖,𝑡,ℎ for trading pair 𝑖 from hour 𝑡 after a pattern emerges to hour 𝑡+ℎ inclusive. To comprehensively assess short-term returns, we consider multiple holding periods, specifically ℎ={1,2,3,6,9,12,15,18,24} hours. This procedure is similar to that of [Ho et al. (2021)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#b25) who choose 2 different holding periods of three and ten hours only though. We aim to provide a more nuanced view on short term returns after candlestick patterns materialize. Our choice hence not only captures anecdotal price reaction, but covers a variety of holding period returns up to a relatively ’long’ period of one day, i.e. 24 h after the arrival of the candlestick pattern. While some studies additionally assess candlestick pattern performance using win rates or hit ratios (e.g., [Cohen, 2021](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#b12), [Huang et al., 2019](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#b28)), we focus on average holding period returns and their statistical significance to quantify the economic magnitude of predictability. A joint analysis of average returns and win rates would constitute a natural extension of our framework and is left for future research.

Next, we compare the returns obtained from a trading strategy based on each pattern against two benchmarks. Firstly, holding period returns are contrasted with (the trivial benchmark of) null returns, i.e., not investing altogether. Secondly, we compare them with the average returns of all cryptocurrency trading pairs on all exchanges in our sample. Unfortunately, direct access to CRIX [3](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#fn3) data, which could serve as a proxy for the overall cryptocurrency market, was not feasible due to its daily frequency. Therefore, we use the simple average of returns of all trading pairs in our sample as a benchmark.

It is important to note that in our analysis, we focus specifically on the relationship between individual returns from trading candlestick patterns and benchmark returns without incorporating trading costs for at least two reasons. Firstly, the omission of trading costs in this context allows us to isolate the impact of candlestick patterns on returns, providing a clear view of their potential profitability relative to benchmark performance. While trading costs are a significant consideration in real-world trading scenarios, our objective at this stage is to assess the raw performance of the candlestick patterns themselves. Secondly, the dataset covers a total of 35 centralized cryptocurrency exchanges with highly heterogeneous fee schedules. For instance, fee schedules differentiate between liquidity maker and liquidity taker, furthermore, individual total trading volume over a period of usually 30 days impacts a trader’s effective trading cost. We will address this issue briefly in the limitations of the study below. However, future studies may consider the incorporation of transaction costs to provide a more comprehensive evaluation of the practical viability of these trading strategies.

### 4.4. Superior predictive power of patterns

The issue of data-snooping bias becomes particularly significant when a large number of hypotheses are tested on the same time series. In the context of this study, a large number of candlestick patterns are identified and subsequent trading returns are analyzed within one time series of OHLC price data, hence, we face exactly the conditions mentioned before. Currently, one effective algorithm known for mitigating data-snooping bias is the Superior Predictive Ability (SPA) test, conceptualized by [Hansen (2005)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#b22). Unlike the less potent and more sensitive White’s Reality Check ( [White, 2000](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#b48)), SPA enhances the adjustment for data-snooping by comparing the performance of a benchmark model against a number competing / alternative forecasting models. This improvement involves studentizing the test statistic and introducing a re-centered sample-dependent null distribution, employing bootstrap methodology. In the context of the null hypothesis (H0), SPA establishes that the benchmark is not inferior to any alternative forecast, offering a robust method for addressing data-snooping concerns in time series analysis.

The significance of data-snooping issues in extensive hypothesis testing empirical studies is further documented by academic efforts on improving and refining the tests mentioned above. So it happened that both, White’s Reality Check as well as the Superior Predictive Ability test have been modified to a stepwise version respectively, see [Romano and Wolf (2005)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#b45) and [Hsu et al. (2010)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#b27) for further details. Most recently, [Li et al. (2021)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#b31) proposed a test on the conditional Superior Predictive Ability of competing models. For the purpose of this paper, we adopt [Hsu et al. (2010)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#b27)’ stepwise SPA test in order to assess the relative predictive power of the numerous candlestick patterns under consideration. It is worth noting that the stepwise SPA test by [Hsu et al. (2010)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#b27) was specifically designed for assessing the predictive abilities of technical trading rules while avoiding data snooping issues, which is why we deem this approach suitable for our objectives.

Here, we provide a brief overview of the stepwise SPA test; for more comprehensive details, please refer to [Hansen (2005)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#b22), [Hsu et al. (2010)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#b27), and [Duvinage et al. (2013)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#b15). The original SPA test defines a loss function for 𝑚 competing models (i.e., candlestick patterns) and a benchmark, represented by market returns or null returns during non-investment periods. The profit 𝜋𝑘,𝑡 obtained from trading pattern 𝑘 at time 𝑡 is expressed as 𝜋𝑘,𝑡=𝛿𝑘,𝑡−1⁢𝑟𝑡, where 𝛿 is set to 1 (−1) for a long (short) position based on the bullish or bearish nature of the candlestick signal. The loss function 𝐿 is then defined as 𝐿⁡(𝑟𝑡,𝛿𝑘)=−𝜋𝑘,𝑡. Further, defining 𝑑𝑘,𝑡=𝐿0,𝑡−𝐿𝑘,𝑡 for 𝑘=1,…,𝑚 and assuming 𝜆𝑘=𝐸⁡(𝑑𝑘,𝑡) lead to the null hypothesis of the SPA test. In essence, 𝐿0,𝑡 represents the benchmark’s loss function, making 𝑑𝑘,𝑡 the relative performance, i.e., the difference between the benchmark and pattern 𝑘’s loss function at time 𝑡. The expected value of 𝑑𝑘,𝑡, denoted by 𝜆𝑘, forms the basis for the null hypothesis: (9)𝐻0:max𝑘=1,…,𝑚⁡𝜆𝑘≤0

This null hypothesis posits that the benchmark is not inferior to any alternative candlestick-based trading rule. The empirical implementation of the SPA test relies on a bootstrapping procedure, ultimately yielding a 𝑝-value for the test. A high 𝑝-value suggests that there is no rejection of the null hypothesis, indicating that none of the 55 candlestick-based trading rules outperforms the benchmark, or conversely, at least one does. However, our interest lies in identifying patterns that surpass the benchmark. This is where the stepwise SPA test becomes relevant. [Hsu et al. (2010)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#b27) propose a three-step procedure to identify trading rules that outperform the benchmark and distinguish those that do not. In our empirical analysis, we utilize the python arch library ( [https://arch.readthedocs.io/](https://arch.readthedocs.io/)) to implement the stepwise SPA test.

Overall, our results reveal both frequent and infrequent patterns exhibiting a wide dispersion of trading performance, ranging from significant losses to substantial gains. These differences in frequency and profitability underscore the importance of a careful real-world implementation of trading strategies based on candlestick patterns. In particular, practitioners must balance the profitability of individual patterns against their frequency of occurrence, while incorporating insights from unprofitable patterns to inform effective risk mitigation strategies. In the next section, we thoroughly discuss the numerical results in more detail.

**Trading returns**

In the subsequent phase, we examine the outcomes within the ensuing 24 h following the identification of a pattern. The concept of reversal patterns posits that a shift in trend occurs following the recognition of such a pattern. Specifically, a bullish reversal pattern is indicative of a transformation from a downtrend to an uptrend, while conversely, a bearish reversal pattern signals a transition from an uptrend to a downtrend. [Fig. 1](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#fig1) presents a summary of cumulative returns during the 24 h following each appearance of a bullish reversal pattern, while [Fig. 2](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#fig2) depicts the cumulative returns associated with bearish reversal patterns. In a nutshell, we had expect positive (negative) cumulative returns throughout for bullish (bearish) reversal patterns.

[Fig. 1](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#fig1), [Fig. 2](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#fig2) illustrate that the anticipated trend reversal effect is observable in approximately only half of the patterns for both bullish and bearish reversal categories. The results exhibit a considerable degree of variability, precluding definitive conclusions regarding the overall efficacy of predicting price reversals using candlestick reversal patterns.

Nevertheless, adopting a more nuanced perspective, particularly regarding the outcomes derived from bullish reversal patterns ( [Fig. 1](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#fig1)), unveils that the preeminent share of the total patterns identified in the dataset pertains to those accurately forecasting the price trend. Upon aggregating the instances of accurate predictions across diverse candlestick formations, it is evident that 5.25 million patterns, constituting 59% of the total 8.96 million, effectively – on average – signal subsequent price movements. When investigating the dominant patterns (Hikkake, Harami), we observe that those correctly predict future price dynamics without exception.

In the context of bearish patterns, our examination reveals a contradiction between the actual returns and the predicted outcomes in 11 out of 25 instances ( [Fig. 2](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#fig2)). Additionally, we observe that cumulative returns for specific patterns, including Harami, Hikkake, and Doji Star, unlike their corresponding bullish patterns do not undergo a change in direction within a 24-h timeframe. This suggests a persistent rather than short-lived correct effect of the pattern lasting up to 12 h only for some bullish patterns. Nonetheless, when employing a frequency-weighted analysis akin to that used for bullish patterns, we find that 80% of the aggregate 5.95 million instances, or 4.77 million cases on average, align with the predictions of the respective bearish patterns.

The graphs related to bearish reversal patterns raise the possibility that the results may be influenced by the cryptocurrency market’s surge during the period of the data set, including the substantial increase of market prices starting in early 2020, often referred to as the ’DeFi-summer.’ To refine our analysis, we adjust the returns for the benchmark of average cross-sectional returns as detailed in the preceding section. [Fig. 3](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#fig3), [Fig. 4](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#fig4) present the benchmark-corrected cumulative returns following the emergence of respective patterns, revealing minimal changes. The findings reiterate the lack of anticipated trend reversal effects within the specified subgroups for both bullish and bearish patterns. We acknowledge that the benchmark correction slightly weakens the results for bullish patterns, with fewer patterns aligning with the desired trend post-appearance, and their impact being less pronounced. Conversely, this is not the case for bearish patterns. Post-correction for the benchmark, the results strengthen, indicating that a greater proportion of bearish patterns exhibit the desired trend direction or more pronounced expressions. However, it is imperative to note that this improvement is only evident in approximately half of the bearish patterns.

Subsequently, we will discuss results for returns relative to the benchmark of null returns in greater detail. The respective results for the benchmark of average market returns do not differ qualitatively.

The stepwise SPA test is an iterative procedure which first ranks all competing trading strategies (i.e., candlestick patterns) in descending order based on the test statistic derived from the traditional SPA test. Those patterns whose test statistic exceeds the threshold (we choose 5% as well as 10%) are being excluded from the sample. Assume that in the first step, a specific number of patterns are rejected. Then, in the second step, the remaining set of patterns are the new data inducing a modification of the distribution the test statistic. Ultimately, the test stops if none of the patterns left are rejected. In conclusion, the stepwise SPA test therefore, preventing data-snooping, identifies the set of candlestick patterns that outperform the benchmark relative to all other competing patterns. This procedure is repeated for all holding periods under consideration, i.e., 1 h to 24 h. Superior trading patterns – at a confidence level of 10% and 5% – are labeled with an ‘x’ in the respective tables.

This leaves a set of tentatively profitable patterns, including bullish Doji Star, Inverted Hammer, Stick Sandwich Bullish, bearish Hanging Man, and, depending on the holding period, bearish Harami and Hikkake patterns. In total, 55 candlestick patterns were tested for nine different holding periods, resulting in nearly 500 pattern-holding-period combinations. The analysis concludes that only a few of these combinations demonstrate real-world profitability, indicating that a substantial portion of candlestick patterns either inaccurately predicts future price trends or fails to outperform the benchmark.

**Summary of baseline results**

The empirical results reveal substantial heterogeneity in the performance of candlestick reversal patterns, which can be explained by their underlying structural characteristics. Across both bullish and bearish patterns, those exhibiting strong and persistent trading returns tend to encode clear and decisive reversals in short-term price dynamics, such as pronounced rejection of prior price levels, gap-based reversals, or large candle bodies. On the bullish side, this includes patterns such as the Inverted Hammer, Doji Star Bullish, Stick Sandwich Bullish, Matching Low, Kicking by Length Bullish, and the rare but highly profitable Three Stars in the South. On the bearish side, similarly strong effects are observed for the Hanging Man, Doji Star Bearish, Harami and Hikkake variants, Kicking Bearish, and the Stalled Pattern Bearish.

A second group of patterns displays predictive power mainly at short horizons, with returns fading over time. These patterns typically reflect price compression or gradual weakening of the prevailing trend rather than forceful reversals. Examples include Harami-based, Hikkake-type, and Tristar patterns on the bullish side, as well as the Evening Star, Dark Cloud Cover, Engulfing Bearish, and Three Inside/Outside Down formations.

Finally, several frequently occurring patterns exhibit weak or systematically adverse performance, likely due to their susceptibility to noise in high-frequency cryptocurrency markets. This group includes bullish patterns such as the Morning Star, Engulfing Bullish, and Abandoned Baby Bullish, and bearish patterns such as Two Crows, Three Black Crows, Breakaway Bearish, and Shooting Star.

Overall, the results indicate that the effectiveness of candlestick patterns depends critically on the strength and clarity of the price action they encode, highlighting the importance of distinguishing among patterns rather than treating candlestick signals as a homogeneous class.

Our findings are closely related to the works of [Anghel, 2021](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#b3), [Cohen, 2021](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#b12) and [Ho et al. (2021)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#b25), who specifically investigate candlestick patterns in cryptocurrency markets. Broadly speaking, these studies document the limited predictive ability and profitability of trading strategies based on technical rules, which is consistent with our results, showing that only a small number of patterns exhibit promising predictive power. Using a large sample of 861 cryptocurrencies, [Anghel (2021)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#b3) conclude that trading rules “are not generally capable of earning abnormal returns,” a finding that closely aligns with ours. Similarly, for a sample of 23 cryptocurrencies and 68 candlestick patterns, [Ho et al. (2021)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#b25) report that technical rules “are of little use in cryptocurrency trading.” Likewise, [Cohen (2021)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#b12) show that in Bitcoin data, only one out of three candlestick patterns appears to be effective in predicting price trend shifts. Interestingly, that pattern is the Engulfing formation, which in our analysis fails to correctly predict price direction. This divergence possibly highlights a broader research gap in the literature, as evidence on candlestick patterns in cryptocurrency markets remains largely anecdotal, with only a small number of studies and limited overlap in the patterns examined. Moreover, meaningful comparisons across studies are difficult, as they differ substantially in terms of data frequency, technical indicators, asset classes, and sample periods. While a few patterns in our analysis are also considered in prior work, the results are not consistent (such as [Cohen, 2021](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#b12)) As an additional example highlighting the heterogeneity of findings, [Caginalp and Laurent (1998)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#b10), using daily equity data from several decades ago, document profitability for patterns such as the Three Black Crows, Morning Star, Three Inside Up, and Three Outside Up, while we find no robust predictive power for these formations. A plausible explanation is that high-frequency data are subject to stronger microstructure noise and rapidly evolving order flow, which may erode pattern-based signals that appear more stable at lower frequencies. This suggests that the effectiveness of candlestick patterns may be contingent on the sampling frequency, an aspect that remains underexplored and warrants further investigation. Expanding on this evidence, [Day et al. (2021)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#b14) study high-frequency data on Bitcoin futures and find that the profitability of bullish and bearish candlesticks depends strongly on the exit strategy, while showing that stop-loss orders do not enhance candlestick-based trading in this market. Taken together, the evidence suggests little support for the profitability of candlestick patterns in cryptocurrency markets, with most studies pointing toward their limited practical value. Comparable results are found in equity markets; for instance, [Duvinage et al. (2013)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#b15) analyze candlestick patterns for Dow Jones constituents and conclude that their predictive power is also too weak to be of practical use. A more promising direction appears to be the integration of machine learning techniques into trading strategies for cryptocurrency markets. The works of [Alkhodhairi et al. (2021)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#b1) and [Alonso-Monsalve et al. (2020)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#b2) illustrate how (deep) neural networks can enhance the performance of technical trading rules, demonstrating improved predictability of price movements to a certain extent. The literature consistently shows that candlestick-based strategies alone have limited predictive and profit-generating potential in cryptocurrency and equity markets alike. However, the incorporation of machine learning methods appears to offer more robust opportunities for improving forecasting accuracy and trading performance, which might be an avenue for future research.

### 5.2. Robustness of results

In this section, we thoroughly assess the robustness of the baseline findings by systematically varying multiple parameters in the analyses outlined in the preceding section.

**Longitudinal sample splits**

First, we examine the temporal and market phase sensitivity of our results. Given our extensive sample period of 3.5 years, which encompasses diverse market conditions such as the consolidation phase following the 2017 bull market, the DeFi-Summer of 2020, the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic, and the robust bull market in the latter half of 2021, the cryptocurrency market exhibits varied characteristics. Consequently, the outcomes of trading candlestick patterns may vary over time.

To address this, we partition our data sample into 21 subsamples, each spanning 1464 consecutive trading hours, equivalent to approximately 61 days or two months per interval. Analyzing each subsample independently reveals results strikingly akin to the baseline outcomes. Specifically, the holding period returns for both bullish and bearish patterns consistently show no significant deviation from those observed in the overall sample. In other words, we observe consistent average price trends over the 24 h following the emergence of candlestick patterns. From this perspective, it appears that candlestick patterns induce similar market reactions, largely irrespective of the prevailing market conditions. However, certain intervals, such as 11, 12, 15, and 16 (roughly corresponding to the mid-2020 to mid-2021 period), consistently exhibit positive returns for the majority of both bullish and bearish patterns. [Fig. 5](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#fig5), [Fig. 6](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#fig6), [Fig. 7](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#fig7), [Fig. 8](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#fig8) illustrate these findings, benchmarking selected patterns against null returns and average market returns for each of the 21 sub-periods.

While the sign of holding period returns is largely the same after bullish and bearish reversal patterns – and hence substantiating the baseline findings – only after correcting for average market returns, subsample results provide a consistent pattern in the holding period, i.e. holding period returns with mainly the same sign. This prompts the assumption that the positive holding period returns for bullish as well as bearish patterns might originate from generally increasing prices in the market in these intervals. We therefore conclude that particularly bearish patterns only underperform the market, while their absolute return is often positive and hence contradicting these patterns’ predicted price development. This lets us further refine the findings from the previous section: apart from holding period constraints, bearish patterns do not work well as trading rules in times of an optimistic market environment.

In a comprehensive examination of holding period returns across 21 distinct subsamples, it is observed that certain bullish patterns consistently yield positive returns in nearly all subsamples, supporting the baseline findings. Notably, the Bullish Doji Star, Harami (Cross) Pattern, and (Modified) Hikkake exhibit this trend, with even more pronounced results observed for the Homing Pigeon, Inverted Hammer, Matching Low, Stick Sandwich, Tasuki Gap, and Tristar Pattern. Upon adjusting these returns in relation to the benchmark of average market returns, the instances of anticipated positive returns are further augmented across subintervals. Conversely, bearish patterns frequently manifest the expected price effect of a substantial downtrend across all subsamples. This is particularly evident for the Bearish Doji Star, Hanging Man, Harami (Cross) Pattern, (Modified) Hikkake, and Stalled Pattern. However, correcting raw holding period returns associated with bearish reversal patterns against the benchmark of average market returns markedly amplifies the occurrence of subintervals featuring the anticipated holding period return patterns. In summary, our analysis does not reveal compelling evidence that the baseline results are predominantly influenced by the overall state of the aggregate cryptocurrency market, as consistent findings are observed across each of the 21 two-month subperiods. Another temporal aspect related to our results concerns the well-documented intraday seasonalities in cryptocurrency markets. [4](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#fn4) Several papers (e.g., see [Baur et al., 2019](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#b5), [Ben Omrane et al., 2023](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#b6), [Brauneis et al., 2024](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#b9), [Dyhrberg et al., 2018](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#b16), [Eross et al., 2019](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#b18), [Hansen et al., 2024](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#b23), [Jasiak and Zhong, 2024](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#b29), [Petukhina et al., 2021](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#b43), [Su et al., 2022](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#b46)) document distinct time-of-day patterns in key market variables such as returns, volatility, trading volume, and liquidity. In particular, there is consistent evidence that these variables vary systematically over the course of a trading day. Our trading strategy could in principle be refined by incorporating additional rules that condition on the time of day—for example, by restricting or enabling trades during certain periods. For reasons of brevity, we have chosen not to include such an extension here and instead leave this as a promising avenue for future research.

**Volatility and trading volume based sample splits**

Second, the comprehensive dataset, encompassing a total of 1935 trading pairs, is partitioned along two distinct dimensions: average trading volume denominated in US dollars and the standard deviation (volatility) of the respective trading pair returns. Subsequently, three subgroups are formed, each comprising 645 trading pairs, categorized based on both trading volume and volatility—classified as low, medium, and high. The analyses, as detailed in the baseline section, are then rerun to examine the profitability and predictive accuracy of individual candlestick patterns. Once again, the results largely align with those obtained in the baseline analysis. Consistent patterns emerge in terms of holding period returns, the temporal distribution of returns within holding periods, as well as the outcomes of t-tests and the SPA test, reinforcing our initial findings from the entire dataset. It is noteworthy, however, that the frequency of observed patterns exhibits notable variations across the trading volume subsamples. Bullish patterns tend to manifest more frequently in the low trading volume tier, while bearish patterns are concentrated within the high trading volume subsample. Turning our attention to the volatility subsamples, we identify above-average occurrences of candlestick patterns in the medium tier, encompassing both bullish and bearish patterns.

**Bitcoin based sample splits**

Third, we execute a partition of the dataset, distinguishing between trading pairs involving the dominant coin in the market, namely bitcoin, and those that do not. The dataset encompasses a total of 872 trading pairs featuring bitcoin, in contrast to 1063 pairs involving coins and tokens other than bitcoin. Our analysis does not yield results diverging significantly from the baseline findings. Consequently, the efficacy of candlestick pattern-based trading appears consistent across all pairs, regardless of the presence or absence of bitcoin.

**The role of outliers**

Fourth, we explore whether the collective outcomes derived from 30 bullish and 25 bearish candlestick patterns are influenced by specific trading pairs within the dataset. To address this, we gather the 100 largest absolute returns associated with each candlestick pattern. These returns contribute to the overall average holding period return for both bullish and bearish reversal patterns. Subsequently, we tally the occurrences of a particular trading pair contributing to this set of remarkably high returns. Employing a chi-squared test with the null hypothesis positing an equal contribution of trading pairs to outlier returns, we find that the null hypothesis cannot be rejected. Consequently, we infer that the observed results are not distorted by the influence of individual trading pairs.

**Relaxing the trend requirement**

Fifth, we thoroughly explore the role of the trend requirement (outlined in Section [4.2](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#sec4.2)) we have imposed on our trading strategy. Motivated by [Heinz et al. (2021)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#b24), who find that the inclusion of trends does not statistically impact the results from trading on the Engulfing pattern in the SP500 index, we revisit our findings without considering the trend requirement. [5](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#fn5) Specifically, we first re-run the pattern recognition process and, unsurprisingly, find a substantially larger number of pattern occurrences (＋130% with bullish patterns, +168% with bearish patterns). Second, we re-assess the superior predictive ability for bullish and bearish pattern utilizing the SPA test. Results show that fewer (more) bullish (bearish) patterns remain superior. Third, when it comes to the profitability of trading on specific patterns, the exclusion of the trend requirement markedly deteriorates the results. That is, bullish patterns’ average trading returns (irrespective of the benchmark; zero or market), a lower than those derived from trading with the trend rule imposed. In addition, bearish patterns’ average trading returns in general are less negative (irrespective of the benchmark; zero or market), implying lower profits from the short position taken upon arrival of a bearish pattern. [6](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#fn6) Hence, the inclusion of the trend requirement appears to be worthwhile in cryptocurrency markets.

**Trading with a stop-loss order**

Sixth, we address the specific choice of trading strategy employed. Our results are based on patterns identified at the closing price of a given trading hour, with trades initiated at that price. Positions are then closed at one of nine subsequent hourly intervals (1, 2, 3, 6, 9, 12, 15, 18, or 24 h), without reference to intra-period highs or lows. Once a bullish or bearish pattern emerges, it provides a directional price prediction. If a bullish (bearish) pattern fails to produce the expected upward (downward) movement, it may be prudent to incorporate a stop-loss order to limit losses from such ’false positives.’ [7](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#fn7) To test this, we implement the following stop-loss mechanism: upon the occurrence of a pattern, we calculate the standard deviation of returns from the past 24 h. If, following a bullish pattern, the price falls by more than two standard deviations, or if, following a bearish pattern, the price rises by more than two standard deviations, the stop-loss is triggered. In such cases, the return is locked in at the extreme low (for bullish) or high (for bearish) that breached the 2 standard deviations threshold, rather than at the scheduled closing price after 𝑥 hours. Results show that, for bullish patterns, irrespective of the benchmark (zero or market returns), stop-loss orders almost always reduce average returns. For bearish patterns, the impact is mixed: six of the ten dominant patterns perform worse under a stop-loss, but the Harami Cross Bearish shows anecdotal improvement, while the Hanging Man, Harami Bearish, and Hikkake Bearish patterns perform better. In summary, stop-loss orders do not improve the profitability of bullish candlestick strategies, although they may add value for select bearish patterns.

**Trading with a liquidity threshold**

Seventh, we revisit the role of market liquidity with our results. [8](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#fn8) Following [Brauneis et al. (2021)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#b8), we adopt the [Corwin and Schultz (2012)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#b13) spread estimator to assess market liquidity beyond trading volume. The estimator utilizes high and low price data and derives an estimate for the quoted spread of a certain trading pair. Specifically, when a qualifying pattern is identified, we compute the ( [Corwin & Schultz, 2012](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#b13)) bid–ask spread estimator using the high and low prices from the previous 24 h, and we only take a position if the estimated spread does not exceed 1.00%. This choice for the estimated quoted spread removes insufficiently liquid assets and retains those with quoted spreads similar to NYSE and NASDAQ stocks according to a recent NYSE report (see [https://www.nyse.com/article/stocks-trade-better-on-nyse](https://www.nyse.com/article/stocks-trade-better-on-nyse)). Consequently, we are able to assess whether the results are driven by liquidity as measured by that estimator. [9](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#fn9) The SPA tests for bullish patterns under the liquidity constraint suggest that the baseline analysis is partly driven by less liquid stocks, as some previously significant results disappear. Nonetheless, the dominant patterns from the baseline calculations persist: the Doji Star and the Inverted Hammer continue to demonstrate strong performance even in the high-liquidity settings For bearish patterns, the SPA tests indicate an overall increase in significance. With only two exceptions, excluding illiquid trading pairs strengthens the performance of trading patterns. Trading returns, after incorporating the liquidity constraint, largely retain both statistical significance and the correct directional sign. However, the magnitude of returns diminishes. For example, in the bullish case (benchmark zero), the Doji Star and Inverted Hammer yield average returns of approximately 12 and 17 basis points, respectively, compared to 35 and 34 basis points in the baseline results. A similar reduction in absolute returns is observed for other bullish as well as bearish patterns when considering only highly liquid pairs. Despite this decrease in magnitude, the overall findings remain robust.

In summary, the outcomes of a wide variety robustness tests do not reveal substantial deviations from the baseline findings. Therefore, the conclusions drawn in the preceding section remain applicable.

### 5.3. Limitations

Similar to any empirical investigation, our findings in this study are subject to certain limitations, which we briefly delineate here. We utilized hourly data encompassing 1935 cryptocurrency trading pairs obtained from 36 exchanges, constituting a substantial dataset. However, we recognize the potential influence of the chosen hourly data frequency on our results. It is essential to note that our findings may not be directly comparable with studies utilizing different data frequencies, such as daily or 5-minute resolution data. Nonetheless, our focus on addressing the research gap regarding the efficacy of candlestick patterns at an hourly resolution contributes novel insights.

Additionally, our analysis incorporates nearly all reversal patterns from the TA-lib Python library. While this encompasses a comprehensive set, the broader field of technical analysis offers an array of tools for accurate price forecasting. Therefore, extending this analysis to include continuation candlestick patterns, as well as exploring trading rules based on moving averages and various indicators, represents avenues for future research. Moreover, combining and employing multiple tools concurrently, rather than exclusively investigating candlesticks, moving averages, or other technical trading rules, remains an area for further exploration.

Crucially, our study does not explicitly consider transaction costs incurred by real-world traders, a factor that could significantly impact the distinction between a profitable and an unprofitable trading strategy. Although we briefly addressed this concern alongside baseline analysis results, it is imperative to recognize that exchanges in our dataset impose varied fees, ranging from 10 basis points (e.g., Binance, as of February 2024, [https://www.binance.com/fee/trading](https://www.binance.com/fee/trading)) to 60 basis points (e.g., Coinbase, as of February 2024, [https://pro.coinbase.com/fees](https://pro.coinbase.com/fees)) for liquidity makers in the lowest trading volume tier. These fees can decrease to as low as 1 basis point, contingent on a client’s actual 30-day trading volume. Consequently, the effectiveness of candlestick patterns in yielding a positive net return is highly contingent on the specific case. Assuming roundtrip trading costs of 20 basis points, certain patterns in our study may prove unprofitable due to fees, while others may still demonstrate viability in the presence of transaction costs.

Lastly, our results rely on the assumption that trades can be executed at the hourly close price (similarly adopted in [Caginalp and Laurent, 1998](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#b10), [Ho et al., 2021](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#b25)), which in practice requires acting quickly after a pattern has emerged. Clearly, monitoring market prices of thousands of trading pairs across several exchanges in real-time, pattern recognition, and initiating trades is infeasible for a human. An implementation of the trading strategy we have outlined therefore necessarily requires automation. [10](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026002716#fn10) In practice, such a system could operate as follows, assuming funded user accounts at all relevant exchanges: (1) real-time price monitoring via exchange APIs or websockets, (2) hourly detection of candlestick formations among the 55 patterns studied (e.g., using the TA-Lib python library, as in our analysis), (3) automatic submission of market orders via exchange APIs when a qualifying pattern is identified, and (4) automatic closing of the position after the specified holding period. In a real-world setting, many algorithmic trading bots would likely compete for similar opportunities, and our strategy would simply represent one among them. However, whether such a bot would be competitive in a live environment lies outside the scope of our paper.

## 6\. Conclusion

This paper presents empirical findings regarding the efficacy of candlestick reversal patterns in forecasting price movements within the intraday cryptocurrency market. Leveraging a comprehensive dataset encompassing hourly data for 1935 trading pairs over a span of three and a half years, we conduct various tests on the trend reversal capabilities of a total of 55 patterns (30 bullish and 25 bearish) sourced from the TA-Lib python library. Furthermore, we thoroughly explore the potential profitability of employing derived trading strategies.

We have identified nearly 15 million instances of candlestick patterns within the dataset. The occurrence of individual patterns exhibits significant variability; for instance, the bullish and bearish Hikkake pattern manifests more than 3 million times, while other patterns such as Breakaway, Abandoned Baby, and Kicking are observed only in a few thousand instances. Nevertheless, these numbers are deemed sufficient for subsequent statistical analyses of returns associated with trading these patterns. Reversal patterns, both bullish and bearish, are instrumental in predicting shifts in the prevailing trend. In cases where a reversal pattern is observed alongside an existing downtrend (uptrend), we scrutinize the 24-h holding period returns following this pattern. It is noteworthy that less than half of the patterns accurately forecast the subsequent price trend. However, when factoring in the frequency of patterns, approximately 60% of all bullish patterns and 80% of bearish patterns are followed by the expected price development on average. In other words, although the majority of patterns inaccurately predict future prices, a few dominant patterns, based on their sheer frequency, reliably indicate the direction of future returns.

We evaluate holding period returns through diverse methodologies. Initially, returns are compared against two benchmarks: the trivial benchmark of abstaining from an investment, i.e., null returns, and the benchmark representing average market returns. Subsequently, employing the skewness-adjusted t-test, we discover compelling evidence consistently rejecting the null hypothesis of zero holding period returns for 12 bullish and 10 bearish patterns. Recognizing the potential data-snooping issues arising from evaluating numerous trading patterns, we mitigate this concern by implementing the stepwise superior predictability test, which facilitates a relative assessment of competing trading strategies. Overall, our findings reveal that the same 12 (10) patterns consistently outperform their respective benchmarks. In a quantitative evaluation of holding period returns, it is observed that only a limited number of patterns exhibit sufficiently large holding period returns that withstand actual trading fees imposed by trading venues. Particularly, bearish patterns demonstrate sufficiently large average holding period returns ranging from 30 to 65 basis points. Despite roundtrip trading costs approximating 20 basis points, this still results in a positive net profit.

Concluding our analysis, we subject our results to a range of robustness tests, demonstrating their stability across alternative empirical specifications. The baseline findings remain qualitatively unchanged when considering various subsets of the full sample. Specifically, we partition the 3.5-year dataset into 21 consecutive subsamples, each covering a two-month period. In addition, we examine subsamples of trading pairs ranked by trading volume and by return volatility, and we separately analyze trading pairs with and without the dominant cryptocurrency, Bitcoin. None of these robustness checks contradict the results obtained using the full sample. While our analysis focuses on the statistical and economic relevance of predefined, rule-based candlestick patterns, future research could extend our framework by incorporating explicit out-of-sample prediction, portfolio-level backtesting, or machine learning approaches. Such extensions would require additional assumptions regarding signal aggregation, execution, and model selection, and therefore lie beyond the scope of the present paper.

In this paper, we examined 55 candlestick patterns and their associated returns over holding periods ranging from 1 to 24 h. The study revealed the predictive efficacy of numerous patterns. However, when considering factors such as overall performance across diverse holding periods, returns adjusted for trading fees, outcomes from the SPA test, and the prevailing market conditions in general, only a select few patterns retained practical significance. Notably, within bullish patterns, the Doji Star and Inverted Hammer emerged as patterns capable of generating tangible profits in real-world trading scenarios. Conversely, among bearish patterns, the Doji Star and Hanging Man pattern demonstrated trading value. In summary, our research underscores an equivocal outcome for trading strategies centered around candlestick reversal patterns. While fewer than half of the patterns accurately forecast future price movements, challenges such as parameter selection and real-world conditions, including trading fees, further diminish the practical utility of the remaining patterns. Consequently, we present anecdotal evidence supporting the profitability of candlestick patterns as a trading tool. However, the lack of compelling reasons for widespread adoption suggests caution in employing these patterns on a large scale.
